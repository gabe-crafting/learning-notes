What an AI engineer does

The job is building software whose most important component is a model you did not train and cannot fully predict. Everything else follows from that sentence.

AI engineer versus ML engineer versus data scientist

The distinction matters when you read job ads, because titles are used loosely. Read the responsibilities, not the title: "integrate LLM capabilities into our platform" is AI engineering; "improve model accuracy through training" is not.

Why a developer background is an advantage

The hard parts of a production AI system are the parts you already know how to do:

  • Designing an API, a data model and a deployment.
  • Handling failures, timeouts, retries and partial results.
  • Caching, batching, queues, background jobs.
  • Testing, CI, observability, cost control.
  • Security and input validation — which matters much more here, not less.

What is genuinely new is small: prompting, embeddings and retrieval, tool loops, and evaluating something that has no single correct answer. That is a matter of weeks, not years — which is why the transition is realistic.

What you will actually build

Nearly every product in this space is one of a handful of shapes:

Extraction / classificationUnstructured text in, structured records out. Invoices, emails, tickets, CVs. Often the highest-value and least glamorous work.
Question answering over your dataRAG. "Ask our documentation / contracts / knowledge base." The single most common brief.
Assistants and copilotsA chat surface with tools that can read and act inside your product.
Agents / workflow automationMulti-step tasks: triage a ticket, research a topic, migrate a file, draft and send.
Content generationDrafting, summarising, translating, rewriting — usually with a human approving the output.

Notice how much of that is plumbing. A realistic split of a working day: perhaps 10% prompt wording, 90% data pipelines, API design, evaluation, debugging and cost.

This course walks up and down that diagram. The bottom two rows are what beginners obsess over; the top and the cross-cutting row are what makes something shippable.

The uncomfortable truths

  • The model is non-deterministic. The same input can produce different output. Your tests, your error handling and your product design all have to accommodate that.
  • It will be confidently wrong. Hallucination is not a bug that gets patched; it is a property you design around — with retrieval, citations, validation and human review where the cost of error is high.
  • Demos are easy, products are not. A weekend RAG demo answers five questions well. Making it answer 500 real user questions well is most of the job.
  • Latency and cost are product features. A brilliant answer after 40 seconds is often worse than a decent answer in three.
  • "Just use an agent" is usually wrong. Most problems are better solved by a fixed pipeline with one or two model calls. Agents cost more, fail in more ways, and are harder to debug.

How to think about a new brief

Before writing any code, answer these:

  1. Does this need a model at all? A regex, a database query or a rules table is cheaper, faster and testable. Use the model for the part that genuinely needs language understanding.
  2. What does "correct" mean here, and who decides? If you cannot describe a good answer, you cannot evaluate one, and you will iterate forever on vibes.
  3. What is the cost of being wrong? A wrong product recommendation is a shrug; a wrong dosage or a wrong legal clause is a catastrophe. This determines how much validation and human review you need.
  4. Where does the knowledge live? If the answer is in your documents, you need retrieval, not a bigger model.
  5. What is the simplest thing that could work? Start with one call and a good prompt. Add retrieval when the model lacks facts. Add tools when it must act. Add an agent only when the steps genuinely cannot be fixed in advance.

The skills, in the order employers ask about them

  1. Calling model APIs well: streaming, structured output, tool calling, errors, cost.
  2. Prompting as engineering — versioned, tested, not folklore.
  3. Embeddings and retrieval; being able to explain why a RAG answer was wrong.
  4. Agents and tool design, including how to bound them.
  5. Evaluation and observability — the strongest signal that you have shipped something real.
  6. Ordinary backend competence: Docker, a cloud, queues, databases.

What a portfolio has to show

Certificates are close to worthless here; the field moves faster than curricula. Running code is the currency. Lesson 21 specifies three projects in detail, but the shape is: a document Q&A system with citations, an agent that uses real external tools, and something with an evaluation suite and a cost report attached. That third attribute — evidence you measured your own system — is what most candidates lack.

Practice
  1. Find five job adverts for "AI Engineer". List the tools and responsibilities each names, and mark which are in this course. Note how many ask for training models (few) versus integration (most).
  2. Take a product you use daily and write down one feature an LLM could genuinely improve. Then answer the five questions above for it, in writing.
  3. Write a one-paragraph definition of the difference between an AI engineer and an ML engineer, from memory. You will be asked this in an interview.
  4. List three tasks where using an LLM would be the wrong choice, and say what you would use instead.
