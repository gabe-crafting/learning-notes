Open models and fine-tuning

When to run your own weights, when to train on your own data, and — usually — why you should do neither yet.

The decision, up front

ProblemSolution
The model does not know your factsRAG (lessons 8–12). Not fine-tuning.
The model does not follow your formatStructured output (lesson 6). Not fine-tuning.
The model does not follow your instructionsBetter prompting and examples (lesson 5).
You need a consistent tone or style at scalePrompt with examples first; fine-tune if that is not enough.
You need a specialised classifier, very cheap, very fast, very high volumeFine-tune a small model — a legitimate case.
Data must not leave your infrastructureSelf-host an open model.
Per-token cost is genuinely prohibitive at your volumeSelf-host, after doing the arithmetic honestly.
"We fine-tuned it on our docs and it still hallucinates" A recurring and expensive mistake. Fine-tuning adjusts behaviour — style, format, task shape. It is a poor and unreliable way to install knowledge, and it goes stale the moment your documents change. Knowledge goes in the context window at query time.

Open models

"Open weights" means you can download and run the model. Notable families you will meet: Llama (Meta), Mistral and Mixtral, Qwen (Alibaba), Gemma (Google), DeepSeek, Phi (Microsoft), plus many fine-tunes on Hugging Face. Licences vary — some are properly open source, others restrict commercial use or scale. Read the licence before you build a business on one.

Why you would

  • Data residency and privacy — nothing leaves your network. Often the deciding factor in health, defence and some public-sector work.
  • Cost at high, steady volume — a rented GPU has a fixed hourly price, so heavy constant load can be cheaper than per-token billing.
  • Latency — a small model on local hardware can beat a network round trip.
  • Control — no deprecations, no rate limits, no surprise behaviour changes.
  • Fine-tuning freedom — you can train the weights you serve.

Why you usually would not

  • Capability gap. The best open models are strong; the frontier hosted models are usually stronger, particularly on reasoning, long context, tool use and multilingual work.
  • You are now running ML infrastructure. GPUs, drivers, batching, scaling, monitoring, upgrades.
  • The economics only work at scale. A GPU costs money while idle; an API costs nothing.
  • Fewer conveniences — caching, batch endpoints, server-side tools and safety layers are yours to build.

Rule of thumb: parameters × bytes per parameter, plus 20–40% for the KV cache and overhead — and the KV cache grows with context length and concurrency, which is what actually catches people out.

Quantisation (GGUF, AWQ, GPTQ) shrinks weights to 8-, 4- or fewer bits with modest quality loss. It is what makes small-GPU deployment realistic.

Hugging Face

from transformers import pipeline

classifier = pipeline("text-classification", model="distilbert-base-uncased-finetuned-sst-2-english")
print(classifier("This product broke after two days"))    # [{'label': 'NEGATIVE', 'score': 0.99}]

Beyond LLMs, the Hub is where you get embedding models, rerankers, OCR, speech-to-text, translation and small task-specific classifiers. A distilled 100M-parameter classifier at a fraction of a millisecond per item is often a far better answer than an LLM call — remember that this whole field is not only chat models.

Fine-tuning

Approaches

Full fine-tuningUpdate every weight. Expensive, needs serious hardware, rarely necessary.
LoRA / QLoRATrain small adapter matrices, freeze the base. A few hundred to a few thousand examples, one GPU, hours not weeks. This is what "fine-tuning" means in practice today.
Hosted fine-tuningSome providers fine-tune their models for you: upload examples, get a model id back. Simplest path when available.
Preference tuning (DPO and friends)Train on chosen/rejected pairs to shape behaviour. Later-stage work.

The honest process

  1. Build the eval set first (lesson 16). Without it you cannot tell whether fine-tuning helped, and fine-tuning frequently makes things worse in ways you will not notice by eye.
  2. Establish the prompted baseline. Best prompt, best examples, best model. Write the number down.
  3. Only then collect data, train an adapter, and measure against the same set.
  4. Check what you broke. Fine-tuning on one task commonly degrades others — "catastrophic forgetting" — and narrows the model's general ability.
  5. Plan the lifecycle. A fine-tuned model is a versioned artifact you must retrain when the base model updates, when your task shifts, and when your data changes.

When it genuinely pays

  • A narrow, very high-volume task where a small fine-tuned model replaces a large prompted one, cutting cost by an order of magnitude.
  • A format or style that resists prompting — a house voice, a domain-specific output convention.
  • A domain vocabulary the base model handles badly (specialised legal, medical or industrial language).
  • Latency requirements that need a small model to be good enough at one thing.

Notice that all four are about behaviour and economics, never about facts.

Distillation

1. Use a strong model to produce high-quality outputs on thousands of your real inputs.
2. Review and clean them — this step is not optional.
3. Fine-tune a small open model on those pairs.
4. Evaluate the small model against the strong one on your eval set.

A practical route to a cheap, fast, specialised model, and a common production pattern once a task is stable and high-volume. Check the terms of service of the model you are distilling from — some prohibit using outputs to train competing models.

A realistic path

1. Hosted frontier model + good prompt              ← start here, always
2. + retrieval                                      ← for knowledge
3. + structured output, tools, evals                ← for reliability
4. → cheaper hosted model on the easy paths          ← for cost
5. → hosted open model                               ← for cost or control
6. → self-hosted open model                          ← for privacy or scale
7. → fine-tuned small model                          ← for a narrow, proven, high-volume task

Most products stop at step 3 or 4 and are entirely correct to. Steps 5–7 are engineering investments that need a measured reason, not a preference.

Practice
  1. Run an 8B model locally with Ollama and compare it with a frontier model on ten tasks from your eval set. Write down where it holds up and where it collapses.
  2. Do the cost arithmetic for your project: API pricing at your expected volume versus a rented GPU per month. Find the break-even point.
  3. Use a small Hugging Face classifier for a task you were about to give an LLM, and compare accuracy, latency and cost.
  4. Write the prompted baseline number for a narrow task, and state what a fine-tune would have to beat.
  5. Build a 200-example fine-tuning dataset for that task — even if you never train on it, the exercise shows you how much work the data is.
  6. Write a short decision memo: for your project, hosted or self-hosted, prompted or fine-tuned, and why.
