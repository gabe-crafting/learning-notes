Interview questions

What an interviewer is likely to ask about AI engineering, an answer worth saying out loud, and the follow-ups that come after it. Practise saying them, not reading them.

How do embeddings work?

The answer (about 40 seconds)

An embedding is a list of numbers that represents the meaning of a piece of text. You send text to an embedding model and it returns a fixed-length vector — a few hundred to a few thousand numbers. It's trained so that texts with similar meaning come out pointing in similar directions.

That's the whole trick: once meaning is a direction in space, comparing meaning becomes geometry. You measure the angle between two vectors — cosine similarity — and that number tells you how related they are.

It's why semantic search beats keywords. Someone searching "how do I cancel" won't match a page titled "terminating your subscription" — zero words in common — but their vectors land close together.

In practice I embed every chunk once at ingestion and store the vectors in pgvector, then embed the incoming question and ask the database for the nearest ones.

The follow-ups

They askYou say
How does the model learn that? It's trained on pairs of texts that should be related, and pushed to put those close together and unrelated ones far apart. I use them, I don't train them.
So why use keyword search at all? Embeddings are bad at exact tokens: product codes, error numbers, rare names. That's why I run hybrid — vector plus BM25 — and fuse the results. (Then give your recall number.)
How did you pick the embedding model? I measured recall on my own labelled set rather than trusting a leaderboard. Dimension size is a cost/quality trade-off, not automatically better when bigger.
What are the failure modes? Negation is weak: "contract includes indemnity" and "excludes indemnity" embed almost identically. And you must use the same model for indexing and querying, or the vectors aren't comparable — changing it means re-indexing everything.
